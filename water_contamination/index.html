Nitrate is a chemical compound which serves as a primary form of nitrogen for many plants, used to synthesize vital organic compounds. Nitrates are used as fertilizers in agriculture because of their high solubility (how much of a substance can dissolve in another substance) and biodegradability. The main nitrate fertilizers are ammonium, sodium, potassium, calcium, and magnesium salts. In addition to serving as a nutrient, nitrates also act as a signaling molecule in plants, regulating processes such as root growth, flowering, and leaf development.

The benefits of nitrates in agriculture (enhanced soil fertility and crop yields) come at a cost when used in excess; excessive use can lead to nutrient runoff, water pollution, and the proliferation of aquatic dead zones. Aquatic dead zones are created via eutrophication, where nitrates (and other forms of nutrient enrichment) cause algal blooms which deplete oxygen in the water to the point where aquatic life (both plants and animals) cannot survive. Sustainable and responsible agriculture practices are therefore necessary to curb excess nitrate usage, as its importance in nature is evident since it supports the growth and development of plants, but can have drastic and harmful consequences when used in surplus.

In addition to sustainable and responsible nitrate usage, implementing cover crops (particularly winter cover crops) on agricultural land significantly reduces nitrate runoff by capturing excess nitrogen in their biomass, decreasing the amount of soluble nitrate in the soil.

PFAS (per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances) are a broad category of synthetic organofluorine chemical compounds with extremely stable molecular bonds. Due to these stable bonds, PFAS compounds are incredibly difficult to break apart, and they do not degrade in nature, earning the monicker "forever chemicals." First synthesized in 1934, their widespread use began in 1938 with the invention of Teflon; thousands of PFAS compounds have since been created, used in a variety of consumer and industrial products to provide resistances to heat, oil, stains, grease, and water. In Minnesota, the passage of Amara's Law in 2023 prohibits PFAS in certain consumer products right now, encompassing all consumer products by 2032.

In addition to being known as a "forever chemical," PFAS could also be referred to as an "everywhere chemical": PFAS are found in rainwater, groundwater, wastewater, and soil; unsurprisingly, water treatment facilities and landfills have the highest levels of pollution. Additionally, crop uptake and bioaccumulation of PFAS into fish, livestock, and wildlife can act as potential routes of human exposure.

Exposure to PFAS, some of which are carcinogens or endocrine disruptors (i.e. hormone disruptors), has been linked to diseases and health conditions including cancers, ulcerative colitis, thyroid disease, suboptimal antibody response (decreased immunity), decreased fertility, hypertensive disorders in pregnancy, fetal and child developmental issues, obesity, and high cholesterol. Unfortunately, with thousands of PFAS compounds used in various applications, only a few (notably PFOS and PFOA) have undergone comprehensive biological testing, leaving a wide data gap and ongoing threats for potential human exposure.

"Impaired" waterbodies are those which have some sort of contamination because of synthetic compounds, naturally occurring compounds, or both. This map shows impaired waters by main problem, highlighting the general causes of contamination in each waterbody. Additionally, this map identifies areas where there is a full cleanup plan in place, along with areas with at least one contaminant still needs a plan for cleanup.

Each waterbody has a designated use category (such as aquatic consumption or drinking water), and each use category has unique sets of contaminants that the MPCA tests for. As such, not all possible contaminants are tested for each waterbody. It is possible that waterbodies are contaminated with compounds that are not listed.

      Nitrate

      What's on the map

      The map shows nitrate samples taken from 2020 through the latest update of this website.

      We compare results to EPA's drinking water limit for nitrate, 10 mg/L. Water above this level is considered unsafe to drink. Many of the streams and rivers sampled aren't used for drinking water, so a high reading means the water would be unsafe if it were used for drinking.

      How we cleaned the data

      • We removed samples reported as quality control.
      • We removed results reported as "Detected, Not Quantified" or "Not Reported," since they have no usable value.
      • We standardized units and the ways different organizations report detection results.

      Long-term nitrate trend chart (2005–2025)

      Finding the long-term trend of nitrate in different regions of Minnesota is difficult, and any method has flaws. The chart shows the best version of the trend we could produce with the data available. Keep these limits in mind:

      • Which sites are sampled changes every year. Not every site is sampled every year, so the pool of sites behind each year's value shifts. This can bias the results.
      • It doesn't represent all of Minnesota's water. The data comes from long-term monitoring sites, which weren't chosen at random.
      • Weather affects the readings. Wet years wash more nitrate off farm fields and into streams, and dry years do the opposite.

      How we calculated the trend:

      1. We used every sample from 2005–2025 taken at "long-term sites": sites with at least one sample in every year from 2020 to 2025.
      2. For each site, we took the median of its nitrate readings within each month.
      3. For each site and year, we took the median of those monthly values.
      4. For each region and year, we took the median across all of its sites. This is the value plotted.

      We compared several other ways of calculating the trend. This one gave the best balance of being easy to explain and holding up against outliers.

      Because of these challenges, read the chart for the general direction of change, not for specific increases or decreases from one year to the next.

      PFAS

      How water systems are categorized

      Each community water system is placed in a category based on its highest detected PFAS level, compared with EPA's drinking water limits. Water above a limit is considered unsafe to drink.

      PFAS compoundEPA limit
      PFOA4 ng/L (parts per trillion)
      PFOS4 ng/L
      PFHxS10 ng/L
      PFNA10 ng/L
      HFPO-DA (GenX)10 ng/L

      Every system labeled Above limit comes directly from MDH's own categorization.

      These are the limits EPA finalized in 2024. In May 2026, EPA proposed rescinding the limits for PFHxS, PFNA and HFPO-DA while keeping the PFOA and PFOS limits. As of this page's last update, that proposal is not final.

      Other notes

      • MDH updates its data regularly. The map reflects MDH's data as of our latest refresh (the date at the top of this page).
      • Some systems buy their water from another system. They're marked "Purchased" under Water Source and shown with the results of the system they buy from.

      Impaired waterbodies

      This layer shows Minnesota's impaired waters for the 2026 reporting cycle. Every state is required to report its impaired waters under the federal Clean Water Act. We use the draft 2026 list, which Minnesota submitted to EPA on September 28. EPA has not yet approved it, so some listings may change. Learn more about this process from MPCA.

      What "impaired" means

      • It's pass/fail. A water is listed when it fails the state standard for a contaminant. The data doesn't show by how much it failed.
      • Not on the map doesn't mean clean. Many smaller waters haven't been assessed yet. Waters that have been assessed weren't necessarily tested for every contaminant.
      • A cleanup plan isn't a cleanup. On the map, waters are drawn bold if at least one of the problems you've selected still needs a cleanup plan, and faded if every selected problem has an approved plan. The counts and charts follow the same rule. An approved plan doesn't mean the water has improved yet.

      What gets tested

      • Testing depends on how a water is used. Each waterbody is assessed against the standards for its designated uses, such as aquatic life, swimming, eating fish, or drinking water. A contaminant is only assessed where a standard for one of those uses applies. For full detail, see MPCA's assessment guidance (PDF).
      • Nitrate is an example. Minnesota's nitrate standard protects drinking water, so it only applies to waters designated for drinking. Many farm-country streams with high nitrate can't be listed as nitrate-impaired. This is why the Nitrate layer and this layer can tell different stories.
      • Mercury, PCBs and PFOS in fish mean the fish aren't safe to eat in unlimited amounts. They don't mean the water is unsafe to swim in or drink. Most mercury reaches Minnesota's lakes through air pollution, which is why it's so widespread.
      • Unhealthy fish and aquatic insect communities show that a stream's ecosystem is damaged, but not what caused it.
      • Many contaminants aren't on the list at all. A contaminant can only be listed if Minnesota has a surface water standard for it. PFOS is the only PFAS compound assessed, and most pesticides have no standard.

      How we show the data

      • MPCA splits longer rivers into sections, so you may see one river drawn in several different colors.
      • Waterbodies often cross county and district lines. We assign each one to only one county, watershed or district, based on a point inside it, so no waterbody is counted twice.
      • We grouped contaminants into eight categories. When a water has problems in more than one category, the map colors it by its highest-ranked category, using the order below. If you turn categories off in the filters, the water takes the color of its highest-ranked category that's still on.

      Water Contamination in Minnesota

      Data last updated: October 19th, 2026

      These maps display Minnesota's impaired waterbodies, with specific overlays for nitrate, PFAS, and general contamination. This is meant to serve both as a reference for you to learn about what is impairing the waterbodies near you, and to connect you to resources to take action and implement mitigation steps for your water.

      How to use the map

      • Left: the legend and filters. They change with the data layer you pick (nitrate, PFAS, or impaired waterbodies).
      • Middle: the Minnesota map. What it shows depends on the layer and filters you choose.
      • Right: charts and key numbers for the selected layer.

      Changing what's on the map

      • Boundaries (dropdown above the map): view the map by county, watershed, legislative district or congressional district.
      • Data layer (toggle beside it): switch between nitrate, PFAS and impaired waterbodies.
      • Zoom and pan with your mouse, or with the buttons in the map's top-right corner.
      • Hover or click on the data for details. Clicking a county, watershed or district zooms the map to that place.

      Charts and key numbers

      Each chart and key number says how to interact with it. Some update with the filters and some don't. They're all specific to the data layer you've selected.
